\begin{abstract}
We test whether loss landscape topology contains predictive signal for catastrophic forgetting in cross-dataset continual learning. Across 19 image classification architectures (0.3M to 44.7M parameters) and three datasets (CIFAR-100, CUB-200-2011, NWPU-RESISC45), we evaluate six ordered task pairs and measure both retention under naive sequential fine tuning and the marginal benefit of elastic weight consolidation (EWC). A pooled mixed-effects analysis on 114 cross-dataset trajectories shows that the zeroth-order persistent homology feature count, $H_0$, carries a robust pooled predictive signal for Task A retention ($p < 0.001$), with the relationship surviving the inclusion of architecture and capacity covariates; per-pair Spearman correlations on the same data are heterogeneous, so the signal manifests as a pooled-aggregation effect rather than as a uniform per-pair relationship. The same predictors do not remain significant for EWC mitigation benefit under cross-dataset pooling, despite within-dataset $H_0$-EWC correlations observed in prior work; we report this as a boundary condition on the topology-mitigation relationship, not a failure of the broader phenomenon. At larger scale (ImageNet-100), eight production-scale architectures show an observational regime shift in which $H_1$ features dominate the topology signal (Spearman $\rho = 0.93$, $p = 0.0007$), reversing the $H_0$-dominance seen at smaller scales. Together, these results map when and how loss landscape topology contains predictive signal for continual learning: its utility is conditional on dataset regime, scale, and aggregation level.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Catastrophic forgetting remains a central challenge in continual learning \citep{mccloskey1989catastrophic, french1999catastrophic}, particularly when models are updated across heterogeneous datasets. While a large body of work has proposed mitigation strategies such as elastic weight consolidation \citep[EWC;][]{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}, replay methods \citep{rebuffi2017icarl, shin2017continual}, and parameter isolation \citep{rusu2016progressive, mallya2018packnet}, a fundamental question remains open: can we predict, before retraining, which models will forget and which mitigation strategies will be effective?

Recent work has suggested that properties of the loss landscape may provide such predictive signals \citep{li2018visualizing, keskar2017on, garipov2018loss}. In particular, topological summaries derived from persistent homology \citep{carlsson2009topology} offer a way to characterize the structure of basins around trained solutions. Early empirical results indicate that these features can correlate with forgetting behavior and mitigation effectiveness within individual datasets. However, it is unclear whether these relationships generalize across datasets, architectures, and scales.

In this work, we take a step toward answering this question by evaluating topology-based predictors in a cross-dataset continual learning setting. We construct a large-scale empirical study spanning 19 architectures and six ordered task pairs across three datasets, yielding 114 cross-dataset training trajectories. This setting introduces a more realistic and challenging regime, where differences in data distribution amplify both forgetting dynamics and measurement artifacts.

Our findings refine and partially revise the role of loss landscape topology in continual learning:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Topology contains a pooled predictive signal for retention, not a uniform per-pair relationship.} The zeroth-order persistent homology feature count ($H_0$) shows a robust pooled effect on Task A retention under naive sequential fine-tuning ($p < 0.001$) in a mixed-effects model with architecture random intercepts. Per-pair Spearman correlations on the same data are heterogeneous, so the signal manifests at the pooled aggregation level rather than as a uniform per-pair effect.
    \item \textbf{The topology-EWC mitigation relationship is a boundary condition, not a generalization.} Within-dataset $H_0$-EWC correlations observed in prior work do not survive cross-dataset pooling at the scales tested here. We treat this as informative scope-setting on when topology predicts mitigation benefit, not as a refutation of the broader topology-forgetting phenomenon.
    \item \textbf{Topology signals are scale-dependent.} At larger scale (ImageNet-100, $n = 8$ production-scale architectures), we observe a regime shift in which first-order features ($H_1$) dominate the topology signal, reversing the $H_0$-dominance seen at smaller scales. The reversal is reported as observational; we do not claim a causal mechanism.
\end{itemize}

Together, these results reposition loss landscape topology not as a universal predictor, but as a conditional signal whose utility depends on dataset regime, learning dynamics, and evaluation setting.

The contribution of this paper is therefore not a single predictive model, but an empirical map of when and how topology provides useful signal in continual learning. This map clarifies the limits of current approaches and motivates future work on topology-aware mitigation strategies. Code, configurations, and results are released open source.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/Axion-Deep-Labs/persist-topological-forgetting}}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Continual Learning and Catastrophic Forgetting.}
Mitigation strategies for forgetting broadly fall into regularization-based methods \citep{kirkpatrick2017overcoming, zenke2017continual}, replay-based methods \citep{rebuffi2017icarl, shin2017continual}, and architecture-based methods \citep{rusu2016progressive, mallya2018packnet}. EWC \citep{kirkpatrick2017overcoming} penalizes changes to parameters deemed important by the Fisher information matrix, effectively anchoring the network near the Task A minimum. Our work is orthogonal to method development: rather than proposing a new mitigation strategy, we investigate whether pre-training landscape properties predict retention dynamics, and the conditions under which they predict mitigation benefit, in cross-dataset continual learning.

\paragraph{Loss Landscape Analysis.}
\citet{li2018visualizing} introduced filter-normalized random directions for meaningful cross-architecture visualization of loss surfaces. \citet{keskar2017on} linked sharpness (largest Hessian eigenvalue) to generalization. \citet{garipov2018loss} demonstrated low-loss paths between independently trained minima, establishing mode connectivity as a key landscape property. We adopt filter-normalized 2D cross-sections following \citet{li2018visualizing} and compute topological invariants on the resulting surfaces.

\paragraph{TDA in Machine Learning.}
Persistent homology has been applied to neural network analysis in several contexts: characterizing decision boundaries \citep{ramamurthy2019topological}, analyzing weight-space topology during training \citep{rieck2019neural}, studying activation patterns \citep{naitzat2020topology}, and providing stable vectorizations via persistence images \citep{adams2017persistence}. Our work differs in applying persistent homology directly to loss landscape \emph{surfaces} rather than to weight trajectories or activation manifolds. Specifically, we use both Vietoris-Rips (via Ripser) and cubical complex (via GUDHI) persistence on 2D loss grids to extract $H_0$ and $H_1$ features, and we relate these to continual learning dynamics rather than single-task generalization.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Overview}

Our experimental protocol consists of seven phases, each producing data consumed by subsequent phases:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{P1.}] Train Task A to convergence.
    \item[\textbf{P2.}] Compute 5 Ripser landscape slices per architecture.
    \item[\textbf{P2c.}] Compute cubical persistence via GUDHI.
    \item[\textbf{P3.}] Measure forgetting under naive, EWC, and cosine LR schedules.
    \item[\textbf{P4.}] Per-dataset correlation and diagnostic analysis.
    \item[\textbf{P5.}] Per-dataset LOAO predictive model with permutation test.
    \item[\textbf{P6.}] Pooled OLS interaction model with clustered bootstrap.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Class-incremental design.}
Each dataset is partitioned into two disjoint class subsets (Task A, Task B) drawn from the same domain, following the standard continual learning evaluation protocol \citep{kirkpatrick2017overcoming, zenke2017continual, rebuffi2017icarl}. This \emph{class-incremental} design controls for domain shift: observed forgetting is attributable to representational interference between related tasks, not to distribution mismatch between unrelated input domains. Cross-dataset task sequences (e.g., CIFAR-100 $\rightarrow$ satellite imagery) would confound the topology--forgetting relationship with domain gap magnitude and cross-domain feature reuse. We address domain generality at the \emph{experiment} level by evaluating across three diverse domains (natural images, fine-grained birds, remote sensing). Cross-domain task sequences remain an important extension for future work (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Loss Landscape Topology}
\label{sec:topology}

We compute persistent homology on 2D cross-sections of the loss landscape around each converged minimum $\boldsymbol{\theta}^*$, using two complementary methods.

\paragraph{Landscape sampling.}
For each architecture and each of 5 independent random seeds, we generate two random directions $\mathbf{d}_1, \mathbf{d}_2$ in parameter space with \emph{filter normalization} \citep{li2018visualizing}: for each convolutional filter (or weight matrix) $i$, rescale $\mathbf{d}^{(i)}$ so that $\|\mathbf{d}^{(i)}\| = \|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*(i)}\|$. The loss is evaluated on a uniform $50 \times 50$ grid over $[-1, 1]^2$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\alpha, \beta) = \mathcal{L}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^* + \alpha\,\mathbf{d}_1 + \beta\,\mathbf{d}_2\right).
    \label{eq:landscape}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Ripser persistence.}
Let $V = \{v_{ij}\}$ denote the $50 \times 50 = 2{,}500$ grid vertices. Assign each vertex a filtration value $f(v_{ij}) = \mathcal{L}(\alpha_i, \beta_j)$. Connect vertices by 8-adjacency (cardinal and diagonal neighbors). Each edge receives the lower-star weight:
\begin{equation}
    w(u,v) = \max\!\bigl(f(u),\; f(v)\bigr).
\end{equation}
We pass the weighted graph to Ripser \citep{bauer2021ripser} and compute $H_0$ (connected components) and $H_1$ (independent loops) persistence diagrams.

\paragraph{GUDHI cubical persistence.}
As a validation baseline, we compute sublevel-set persistence directly on the $50 \times 50$ loss grid using GUDHI's \texttt{CubicalComplex}. This avoids the graph-construction step and operates on the raw scalar field.

\paragraph{Cross-method agreement.}
$H_1$ total persistence from Ripser and GUDHI shows Spearman $\rho = 1.0$ agreement on all 3 datasets, confirming that the topological measurements are method-independent.

For each homology dimension $k$, we record total persistence $\text{Pers}_k = \sum_{i} (d_i - b_i)$, feature count, and maximum lifetime. Using 5 independent slices per architecture (seeds logged for reproducibility) substantially reduces sampling variance compared to single-slice designs.

\subsection{Forgetting Measurement}

Starting from the Task A checkpoint, we expand the final classification layer and train on Task B under three conditions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Naive:} SGD with momentum 0.9, weight decay $5 \times 10^{-4}$, and fixed learning rate 0.01 (0.001 for ViT variants, which use a lower base rate).
    \item \textbf{EWC:} Naive training plus elastic weight consolidation \citep{kirkpatrick2017overcoming} with diagonal Fisher information matrix ($\lambda = 1000$).
    \item \textbf{Cosine LR:} Naive training with cosine annealing learning rate schedule.
\end{enumerate}

Task A test accuracy is evaluated at steps $\{10, 25, 50, 100, 250, 500, 1000, 5000\}$. Our primary retention metric is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{ret}@k = \frac{\text{acc}_A(\text{step} = k)}{\text{acc}_A(\text{step} = 0)}.
    \label{eq:retention}
\end{equation}
We also compute the area under the retention curve (AURC) via trapezoidal integration over the early evaluation steps (steps 0--500), denoted early\_aurc. The EWC benefit is defined as the absolute improvement in retention:
\begin{equation}
    \text{EWC\_benefit} = \text{ret}_{\text{EWC}} - \text{ret}_{\text{naive}}.
    \label{eq:ewc_benefit}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Full-softmax vs.\ restricted-softmax retention.}
When Tasks A and B come from different datasets, retention computed via full-softmax over the expanded $K_A + K_B$ classifier head reflects two effects: backbone drift (loss of Task A representations) and classifier-head re-routing (Task A logits being eclipsed by inflated Task B logits even when the backbone still encodes Task A correctly). To isolate backbone drift, we additionally compute restricted-softmax retention by evaluating Task A inputs over only the original $K_A$ classifier outputs, holding the Task B head fixed. Our primary Phase 4 and Phase 8 analyses use full-softmax retention; restricted-softmax retention is computed on the same trajectories and reported as a metric verification in Section~\ref{sec:metric_verification}.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:stats}

\paragraph{Per-pair correlation analysis.}
For each of the six ordered cross-dataset task pairs, we compute Spearman rank correlations between topology features ($H_0$, $H_1$) and the retention/EWC-benefit outcomes across the 19 architectures. We apply Bonferroni correction to per-pair tests (adjusted $\alpha = 0.0083$ across six pairs).

\paragraph{Pooled mixed-effects analysis.}
The primary cross-dataset analysis is a linear mixed-effects model fit on all $n = 114$ trajectories (six task pairs $\times$ 19 architectures), with architecture as a random intercept to absorb across-architecture variation:
\begin{align*}
    \text{ret}_{10} &\sim H_0 z + H_1 z + \log(\text{params}) + (1 \mid \text{arch}), \\
    \text{ewc\_benefit} &\sim H_0 z + H_1 z + \log(\text{params}) + (1 \mid \text{arch}),
\end{align*}
where $H_0 z$ and $H_1 z$ are within-trajectory z-scored topology features. Fixed effects are tested with Wald $z$-tests and 95\% confidence intervals; the random-effect variance is tested via the usual likelihood-ratio approximation. We do not include explicit dataset interaction terms in the primary specification, as the random architecture intercept and the within-trajectory z-scoring are sufficient to absorb most of the across-pair scale heterogeneity. Sensitivity analyses with explicit pair indicators yield qualitatively identical results.

\paragraph{Scale-validation analysis (Phase~I-A).}
The ImageNet-100 sweep is analyzed as a separate scale-validation step on $n = 8$ production-scale configurations, using Spearman rank correlations between the topology features and the analogous outcome variable. This analysis is reported as exploratory at the current sample size and does not enter the pooled mixed-effects model.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section reports our cross-dataset findings (Phase~I-B) on retention prediction and EWC mitigation, the contrasting null result for EWC mitigation benefit at the same scale, and the scale-regime shift observed at production scale (Phase~I-A on ImageNet-100). Retention metrics throughout the primary Phase 4 and Phase 8 analyses are computed using full-softmax retention; restricted-softmax retention is computed on the same trajectories as a metric verification (Section~\ref{sec:metric_verification}). The verification confirms that the choice between full and restricted softmax does not affect Phase 4 or Phase 8 conclusions at the paper's primary evaluation step (ret$_{10}$).

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Retention Prediction}
\label{sec:results_q1}

\text{ret}_{10} \sim H_0 z + H_1 z + \log(\text{params}) + (1 \mid \text{arch}),

At this scale, the dominant topology predictor reverses. $H_0$ persistence monotonically decreases with network width ($\rho = -1.0$ vs.\ parameter count) on CIFAR-100, CUB-200, and RESISC-45. This is consistent with the intuition that wider networks have smoother loss landscapes with fewer disconnected basins. Cubical and Ripser $H_1$ values show perfect rank agreement ($\rho = 1.0$), confirming that the topological measurement is method-independent. With only six widths, perfect rank correlation is consistent with a structural relationship but can also occur by chance, and we treat the ladder as supporting evidence for the validity of the $H_0$ measurement rather than as a primary result.

\subsection{Metric Verification: Full-Softmax vs.\ Restricted-Softmax Retention}
\label{sec:metric_verification}

Full-softmax retention conflates two effects in cross-dataset continual learning: backbone drift (loss of Task A representations) and classifier-head re-routing (Task A logits eclipsed by inflated Task B logits even when the backbone still encodes Task A correctly). Restricted-softmax retention isolates backbone drift (Section~\ref{sec:method}). To verify that the choice between these two metrics does not condition the Phase 4 and Phase 8 conclusions reported above, we computed both metrics on the same training trajectories and compared them across 108 of the 114 cross-dataset configurations (18 of 19 architectures, spanning the full 0.3M--36.5M parameter range, across all six ordered task pairs). The six omitted trajectories correspond to ResNet-18, whose training checkpoints were not retained for re-evaluation; the Phase 4 and Phase 8 analyses above use the original full-softmax retention measurements computed when those trajectories were on disk.

At the paper's primary evaluation step (ret$_{10}$), the two metrics are operationally identical: Spearman rank correlation $\rho = 1.0000$ across all 108 verified trajectories, mean absolute difference $0.0001$, maximum absolute difference $0.012$. The choice between full and restricted softmax does not affect Phase 4 or Phase 8 conclusions at this step.

At later evaluation steps the two metrics diverge sharply (Table~\ref{tab:metric_verification}). Pooled max absolute difference crosses the $0.20$ threshold by step 100 and remains elevated through step 1000. The per-pair $\rho$ range reaches $0.703$ at step 100, indicating that the magnitude of divergence depends on which task pair is being measured.

This pattern is consistent with the operational interpretation in Section~\ref{sec:method}. At very early steps, few Task B gradient updates have been applied, so the argmax over the full $K_A + K_B$ classifier head selects Task A classes at the same rate as the restricted-softmax argmax over $[0, K_A)$. As Task B training continues, Task B logits inflate and full-softmax begins to misclassify Task A inputs into Task B classes even when the backbone still encodes them correctly; restricted-softmax preserves backbone-drift signal that full-softmax loses. A full characterization of this temporal and geometric divergence is out of scope for the present paper, which uses the metric verification only to confirm that the headline ret$_{10}$ findings in Sections~\ref{sec:results_q1} and~\ref{sec:ewc_benefit} are insensitive to the choice between full and restricted softmax.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Toward a Mechanism: Basin Fragmentation Hypothesis}
\label{sec:hypothesis}

One possible explanation for the relationship between topological features and retention observed in this study is \textbf{basin fragmentation}. $H_0$ persistence in the sublevel-set filtration counts connected components of low-loss regions; intuitively, landscapes with greater $H_0$ contain more disconnected basins separated by high-loss barriers, while landscapes with low $H_0$ resemble a single broad basin. We hypothesize that fragmented landscapes provide more locally robust solutions that resist drift during sequential training: parameters anchored within one basin are less likely to be pulled across a barrier into a region that encodes only the new task. Smoother landscapes, by contrast, may already be intrinsically robust without requiring topological characterization, which would explain why $H_0$ contributes a pooled predictive signal for retention across heterogeneous cross-dataset task pairs while the same predictor does not generalize to mitigation benefit at the scales tested here.

However, the present study does not test this mechanism directly. The results establish \emph{when} topology contains predictive signal for retention, not \emph{why}. The cross-dataset pooled null on EWC mitigation benefit further indicates that the topology-mitigation relationship is a boundary condition rather than a straightforward generalization of the within-dataset findings.

Future work should investigate whether basin structure causally mediates forgetting dynamics. Promising directions include (i) intervening on loss landscape geometry via sharpness-aware minimization or controlled weight-decay sweeps to change $H_0$ while holding other landscape properties approximately fixed; (ii) tracking basin transitions during sequential training using filtration-curve analysis; and (iii) testing whether the topology--retention relationship persists under non-curvature-based mitigation methods (e.g., replay), which the basin fragmentation account predicts should differ in pattern from EWC. The hypothesis is offered as a candidate mechanism to guide future confirmatory work, not as a claim supported by the present results.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Scope and Design}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Limited architecture range.} The 19 architectures span 0.3M--44.7M parameters. The Phase~I-A scale validation extends to ImageNet-100 with eight production-scale configurations, but production continual learning systems can reach 100M--7B+ parameters. Whether the topological signal patterns reported here persist at the largest scales remains open, and computing persistent homology on very large parameter spaces introduces superpolynomial computational barriers.
    \item \textbf{Two-task sequences.} We test only a single Task A $\rightarrow$ Task B transition. Real continual learning involves 10--100+ sequential tasks. Topology--forgetting dynamics over long task sequences are unexplored in this work.
    \item \textbf{Single mitigation method.} Mitigation analysis is restricted to EWC. The cross-dataset pooled null does not rule out that other mitigation strategies (synaptic intelligence \citep{zenke2017continual}, PackNet \citep{mallya2018packnet}, replay-based methods) might exhibit a topology--benefit relationship that EWC does not, and a negative control with a non-curvature-based method would help separate EWC-specific effects from regularization-general ones.
    \item \textbf{Image classification only.} All datasets are image classification benchmarks with class-incremental task splits. Whether the topology--retention relationship holds on NLP tasks, structured prediction, or cross-domain task sequences is unknown.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Statistical Fragility}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Within-dataset sample sizes are small.} Per-dataset analyses operate on $n = 19$; the WRN width ladder on $n = 6$. Spearman correlations at these sample sizes are sensitive to influential points. The cross-dataset pooled mixed-effects analysis ($n = 114$ trajectories) is more robust, but its random-effects structure relies on assumptions about variance-component independence that we do not formally test.
    \item \textbf{Multiple-testing burden.} Even with Bonferroni correction at the per-test level, the implicit testing burden across the full study (per-dataset correlations, partial correlations, multiple outcome metrics, pooled models, scale-validation comparisons) is larger than any single correction accounts for. The familywise error rate across the entire study is likely understated.
    \item \textbf{High-influence observations.} In the pooled per-dataset OLS analyses, Cook's distance identifies ConvNeXt-Tiny (CIFAR-100) as a high-leverage point ($D = 2.89$). DFBETAS for the $H_0$ coefficient are small ($< 0.03$), but influential points in a 57-observation model still warrant caution.
    \item \textbf{No outlier-robust alternatives reported.} We do not report robust regression or rank-based alternatives to OLS for the per-dataset pooled models.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Conceptual Weaknesses}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{2D slice fidelity.} The topology signal depends on 2D cross-sections of a high-dimensional surface. Five independent random-direction slices reduce sampling variance, but we do not empirically demonstrate that this approximates the true local basin topology. $H_0$ measured on a 2D slice can in principle reflect saddle structure or orientation artifacts rather than true basin multiplicity.
    \item \textbf{$H_0$ and capacity.} $H_0$ is strongly tied to model capacity via the WRN width ladder ($\rho = -1.0$). Per-dataset controlled regressions show that $H_0$ retains significant predictive power on RESISC-45 ($p < 0.001$) after controlling for $\log(\text{params})$, with $H_0$ explaining more variance than parameter count on that dataset. The confound is partially but not fully resolved at small per-dataset sample sizes.
    \item \textbf{WRN perfect monotonicity.} $\rho = -1.0$ across all three datasets with only six widths ($k = 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10$) is consistent with a structural relationship but can also occur by chance.
    \item \textbf{No causal mechanism.} \emph{This study does not establish a causal mechanism linking topology to forgetting.} The relationships reported are correlational, computed from passive observation of trained networks. No experiment intervenes on landscape geometry to test whether $H_0$ changes drive retention changes correspondingly. The basin fragmentation account in Section~\ref{sec:hypothesis} is offered as a candidate hypothesis to guide future work, not as a claim supported by the present data.
    \item \textbf{Restricted-softmax retention is novel and load-bearing.} The full-softmax versus restricted-softmax distinction developed in Section~\ref{sec:method} and verified empirically in Section~\ref{sec:metric_verification} is not yet validated against an established cross-dataset retention literature. The verification confirms operational identity at ret$_{10}$ ($\rho = 1.0000$, $n = 108$) but documents substantial divergence at later evaluation steps ($\rho = 0.63$ at step 1000). We treat the distinction as part of the conceptual contribution: full-softmax retention reflects both backbone drift and classifier-head re-routing, while restricted-softmax isolates backbone drift alone. The temporal and geometric structure of the divergence (Section~\ref{sec:metric_verification}) implies phase-dependent forgetting dynamics that future work should characterize formally and compare against alternative metrics designed for cross-domain continual learning.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Falsification targets.} Results that would undermine the empirical claims of this paper:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The pooled $H_0$--retention relationship fails to replicate on a comparable cross-dataset sweep using restricted-softmax retention with a different architecture set.
    \item Adding 50--100+ architectures reveals a previously hidden interaction term that breaks the cross-dataset pooled $H_0$--retention effect.
    \item Cubical persistence disagrees with Ripser on the pooled mixed-effects result, indicating method dependence rather than landscape signal.
    \item The Phase~I-A $H_1$ dominance pattern at ImageNet-100 scale fails to replicate on a different production-scale dataset.
    \item A replay-based mitigation method shows equal $H_0$--benefit correlation as EWC, indicating that any topology--mitigation relationship is not specific to curvature-based regularization (which would weaken the basin fragmentation account).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We tested whether loss landscape topology contains predictive signal for catastrophic forgetting in cross-dataset continual learning across 19 image classification architectures spanning 0.3M--44.7M parameters and three datasets (CIFAR-100, CUB-200-2011, NWPU-RESISC45). A pooled mixed-effects analysis on 114 cross-dataset trajectories shows that the zeroth-order persistent homology feature count, $H_0$, carries a robust pooled predictive signal for Task A retention ($p < 0.001$), with the relationship surviving as a fixed effect after architecture and capacity are accounted for; per-pair correlations on the same data are heterogeneous, locating the signal at the pooled aggregation level rather than as a uniform per-pair claim. The same predictor does not remain significant for EWC mitigation benefit under cross-dataset pooling, despite within-dataset findings on individual datasets in earlier work; we report this as a boundary condition on the topology-mitigation relationship. At larger scale, eight ImageNet-100 configurations show an observational regime shift in which first-order ($H_1$) features dominate the topology signal (Spearman $\rho = 0.93$, $p = 0.0007$), reversing the $H_0$-dominance observed at smaller scales.

The contribution of this paper is not a single predictive model, but an empirical map of when and how loss landscape topology provides predictive signal for continual learning. The map clarifies that the topology-retention relationship survives as a pooled effect across heterogeneous task pairs, that it does not extend to mitigation benefit at the scales tested here, that the dominant order of topology features depends on model scale, and that the choice of cross-dataset retention metric (full-softmax versus restricted-softmax) materially conditions interpretation. The utility of topology as a predictor of forgetting is conditional on dataset regime, scale, aggregation level, and evaluation metric.

Open questions for future work include: (i) whether the cross-dataset $H_0$--retention pattern persists at production scale (100M--7B+ parameters); (ii) whether topology provides predictive signal for non-curvature-based mitigation methods (replay, parameter-isolation) where it appears not to for EWC at this scale; (iii) whether a causal intervention on landscape geometry, such as sharpness-aware minimization or controlled weight-decay sweeps, demonstrates that $H_0$ changes drive retention changes correspondingly; and (iv) whether long task sequences (10--100+ tasks) produce qualitatively different topology--forgetting dynamics than the two-task setting studied here.
